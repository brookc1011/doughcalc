## Straight doughs (ch. 5)

### Saturday White Bread (p.81)
- A one-day bread: mix in the morning, shape about 5 hours later, bake late afternoon. The book's suggested first recipe.
- Makes 2 loaves, each about 1½ lb. Suitable for pizza or focaccia. With one loaf, the rest makes 2-3 balls for skillet pizza or focaccia, kept in the fridge 2-3 days.

### Overnight White Bread (p.89)
- Rises overnight. Compared with Lahey's no-knead bread: water about 30°F warmer and one-third the yeast, with an autolyse and a couple of folds after mixing.
- Makes 2 loaves, each about 1½ lb. Suitable for focaccia or iron-skillet pizza.
- **Bulk:** 12-14 hours. **Proof:** about 1¼ hours.
- **Sample schedule:** mix 7 p.m., shape 8 a.m., bake 9:15 a.m., out a little after 10 a.m.

| Ingredient | Grams | Volume | Baker's % |
|---|---|---|---|
| White flour | 1,000 g | 7¾ cups | 100% |
| Water | 780 g, 90-95°F | 3⅓ cups | 78% |
| Fine sea salt | 22 g | 1 tbsp + 1 tsp | 2.2% |
| Instant dried yeast | 0.8 g | scant ¼ tsp | 0.08% |

- The extended bulk gives more flavor complexity than the two Saturday breads. Bake well beyond the blond stage for a crisp crust. Water 90-95°F is 32-35°C.

**Method (two pages after p.89, page numbers not shown; photographed 2026-11-13)**
1. **Autolyse.** 1,000 g flour and 780 g water at 90-95°F in a 12-quart round tub or similar. Mix by hand just until incorporated. Cover, rest 20-30 minutes.
2. **Mix.** Sprinkle the 22 g salt and 0.8 g yeast (scant ¼ tsp) evenly over the top. Wet your mixing hand first; rewetting three or four times is fine. Reach under, grab about a quarter of the dough, stretch it and fold it over the top; repeat three more times until salt and yeast are enclosed. Then pincer method: five or six pincer cuts across the whole mass, fold the dough over itself a few times, repeat until fully integrated with some tension. Rest a few minutes, then fold another 30 seconds or until it tightens. **Target dough temperature at end of mix: 77-78°F (25-26°C).** Cover and let rise.
3. **Fold.** Two or three folds (pp.69-70). Three is best for gas retention and volume; two is fine. Easiest to do them in the first 1½ hours after mixing. After the last fold, cover and let it rise overnight **at room temperature**. Ready to divide when it is **2½-3 times its original volume, 12-14 hours after mixing.**
4. **Divide.** Moderately flour a work surface about 2 feet wide. Flour your hands and around the tub edges, tip the tub, work a floured hand under the dough to loosen it, and ease it out without pulling or tearing. Pick it up and ease it back down in a somewhat even shape. Flour the middle and cut into 2 equal pieces with a dough knife or plastic scraper.
5. **Shape.** Dust 2 proofing baskets with flour. Shape each piece into a medium-tight ball (pp.71-73). Seam side down in the basket.
6. **Proof.** Lightly flour the loaf tops. Side by side under a kitchen towel, or each basket in a nonperforated plastic bag. Plan to bake about **1¼ hours after shaping at about 70°F (21°C)**; in a warmer kitchen, about 1 hour. Use the finger-dent test (p.74) and **check after 1 hour**. With this bread 15 minutes can be the difference between perfectly proofed and collapsing a bit.
7. **Preheat.** At least 45 minutes before baking: rack in the middle, 2 Dutch ovens on it with lids on, 475°F (245°C). With only 1 Dutch oven, put the second loaf in the fridge **about 20 minutes** before baking the first, bake them one after the other, and give the Dutch oven a **5-minute reheat** after removing the first loaf.
8. **Bake.** Don't let hands, fingers or forearms touch the hot Dutch oven. Invert the loaf onto a lightly floured counter; the top of the loaf is the seam side. Mitts on, take out the Dutch oven, lid off, loaf in seam side up, lid back on, into the oven at 475°F. **30 minutes lid on, then 20-30 minutes lid off**, until at least medium dark brown all around. Check after 15 minutes uncovered in case your oven runs hot. Tilt the loaf out, cool on a rack or on its side so air circulates, **rest at least 20 minutes before slicing.**

Differences from the general ch. 4 notes (fwsy-general.md): this recipe's lid-off time is 20-30 minutes (general: usually 15-20), the one-Dutch-oven fridge lead is about 20 minutes (general: 15-20), and the method page states the overnight rise is at room temperature, which the intro page did not.

**Variation: Weeknight White Bread (second method page, page number not shown)**

## Understanding levain (ch. 7, from the footer "Understanding Levain" and the pointer to ch. 8 for details; page numbers not shown)

**Levain hydration**
- The book's levain recipes use a culture at **80% hydration** (water = 80% of the flour by weight). The common range is **60-100%**. A 60-65% levain is stiff enough to form a ball.
- The author's bakery keeps the levain close to the hydration of the dough it goes into.
- The levain is a **blend of whole wheat and white flour**.

**Flavor aim**
- The middle of the lactic-to-acetic range: mellow, not sharply sour.

**Variables that change a culture**
- Hydration: wetter favors lactic acid, stiffer favors acetic.
- Temperature: **78-90°F** favors lactic and develops faster. **55-65°F** favors acetic.
- Flour: whole grain, rye, high-extraction and high-ash flours ferment more vigorously and can need more frequent feedings.
- Salt: delays fermentation. The author doesn't salt the levain, though some environments or schedules call for a little salt in it (2nd pass).
- Yeast: **never add commercial yeast to the culture**, to start it or to keep it. A small amount in the final dough is fine as long as the culture stays separate.

**Hybrid leavening**
- Many of the book's levain recipes add store-bought yeast to the final dough (not the culture) for more volume, a lighter crumb and less acidity. Ch. 9 (Hybrid Leavening Doughs) uses levain plus a little commercial yeast. Ch. 10 (Pure Levain Doughs) uses levain only.
- An alternative is a poolish mixed when the levain is fed, both added at the final mix. The author finds it hard to tell from adding a little yeast.

**Levain schedule**
- **One feeding in the morning, about 6-9 hours before mixing the final dough.**
- Between bakes, either feed once each morning or keep a piece of the leftover levain in the fridge and refresh it before the next bake. Details in the next section.

## Levain method (ch. 8, pp.130-137)

Page numbers: p.130 and p.134 come from the book's own cross-references ("page 130" for the starting guide, "page 134" for Seasonal Variations), p.136 shows a partial footer ("36", first digit cut off), and the rest follow page order (left pages even, right pages odd). p.131 is a photo page and wasn't sent.

**Starting a levain from scratch (pp.130-133).** Five days, a few minutes each day.
- Start with whole grain flour (whole wheat is easier to handle than rye). Use a 6-quart round tub with a lid, and reuse it unwashed.
- **Weigh the empty tub first** and write the weight down. From Day 4 on, feedings are measured by tub weight plus levain. The author's tub weighs 410 g, so 100 g of levain reads 510 g.
- Early days don't need exact amounts. Once it's on a maintenance routine, exact flour, water and water temperature matter for consistent results.
- **Day 1, any time before noon:** 500 g whole wheat flour + 500 g water at about 90°F, mixed by hand. Uncovered 1-2 h, then covered, in a warm place. 75-90°F is ideal but not required.
- **Day 2, before noon:** discard about three-quarters (an eyeball is fine), add 500 g whole wheat + 500 g water at 90°F. Same rest. By the end of the day it should be near the 2-quart line with small bubbles.
- **Day 3, before noon:** it should have doubled since the day before, with bubbles throughout and a leathery alcohol smell. Same discard and feed. A "sour porridge" smell later in the day.
- **Day 4, before noon:** back to the 2-quart line. Keep exactly 200 g (weighed against the tub), add 500 g whole wheat + 500 g water at 90°F. Cover, warm place.
- **Day 5, between 7 and 9 a.m.:** keep 150 g, add 400 g white + 100 g whole wheat + 400 g water at 85°F. Cover, warm place. Ready for dough by the afternoon. This is the switch to the ongoing levain: 80% hydration, a white and whole wheat blend, slightly cooler water (2nd pass).
- **Ripeness cue:** 7-8 hours after the morning feed it has a medium-ripe smell, and a wet hand pulling out a chunk feels gas and a web-like structure. Very goopy.

**Using your levain (pp.134-135).**
- Fed each morning without being used, it grows to 3-4 times its volume by the next morning.
- Volume and smell are the cues. Smell at use time carries straight into the bread's flavor.
- The author's kitchen is about 70°F by day and about 65°F at night. A much warmer or colder kitchen may need adjusting.
- **Too sour:** slightly cooler feed water, or mix the final dough earlier in the afternoon when the levain is less ripe.
- **Hot, humid kitchen (about 80°F):** mix the final dough 1-2 hours earlier than the recipe says. If you like a more sour loaf from an extra-ripe levain, you can keep the book's schedule.
- **Much colder kitchen:** feed with water at about 95°F.
- **Target levain temperature right after a feed: 78-80°F.** Measure it and adjust next time's water.
- **Ch. 9 hybrid schedule:** feed in the morning, mix the final dough in the afternoon, divide and shape 5 hours later, proof overnight in the fridge, bake the next morning. A little baker's yeast goes in the dough, never the levain.
- **Ch. 10 pure levain schedule:** feed in the morning, mix early evening, bulk overnight, divide and shape next morning, bake 4 hours later.
- Both use the same feeding, storing and refreshing schedule.
- Hybrid breads: lighter, more volume, thinner crust, milder. Pure levain: a little denser and less domed, bigger holes, chewier crust, more tang.

**Seasonal variations (p.134).** Recipes were tested in every season in Portland.
- In winter the levain is less active. For the pure levain recipes the author adds about 50 g more levain to the final dough; the recipes note this.
- Another winter fix: keep 30-50 g more levain at the morning feed, same fresh flour and water.
- In summer, if the bread is too sour, keep less levain at the morning feed.
- Hybrid doughs vary less by season than pure levain doughs.

**Daily feeding formula (pp.135-136).** Feed each morning at about the same time; an hour or two either way is fine.

| Item | Full | Half |
|---|---|---|
| Levain kept | 100 g (a little more in winter) | 50 g |
| Whole wheat flour | 100 g | 50 g |
| White flour | 400 g | 200 g |
| Water | 400 g, 85-90°F (warmer in winter, cooler in summer) | 200 g, 85-90°F |

- Method: discard all but the kept amount, zero the scale, add the flour, then the water, mix by hand until just combined. Cover and leave at room temperature between feedings.
- Gloves are optional; the culture is acidic.
- After using some in a recipe, leave the rest in its tub at room temperature and feed it as usual the next morning.

**Storing and restoring (pp.136-137).**
- Storage: after a final mix, take about 300 g of the leftover levain, coat it with a film of water, seal it in a non-perforated plastic bag, and refrigerate **for up to 1 month**.
- **Step 1, two days before baking:** put 200 g of the stored levain in the empty bucket and discard the rest. Let it warm 30-60 min if possible. Add 100 g whole wheat + 400 g white + 400 g water at **95°F**. Cover, warm spot, overnight.
- **Step 2, the morning of the day before baking:** the normal daily feed (keep 100 g; 100 g whole wheat, 400 g white, 400 g water at 85-90°F). Rest in a warm spot until the dough mix later that day.
- **Sample schedule for a Sunday-morning bake:** Friday morning refresh (step 1); Saturday morning feed (step 2); Saturday afternoon mix. Hybrid: shape Saturday evening, fridge overnight, bake Sunday morning. Pure levain: bulk overnight, shape Sunday morning, bake around noon.
- Starting from scratch for a Sunday bake: begin Day 1 on the Tuesday before.

## Making a dough your own (essay from p.190)

Page numbers inside the essay aren't shown; the essay starts at p.190 per the Pain de Campagne page.

**The book's own stance.** The recipes are templates to adjust: flour blend, leavening, timing, hydration. Follow them as written first, then make them yours. This matches CJ's rule that the book is the starting point, not the bible.

**Changing hydration.**
- Overnight Pizza Dough with Poolish (p.225) at 70% instead of 75%: total water 700 g, so the final-dough water drops from 250 g to 200 g, same poolish. A stiffer dough needs only one fold. Table as printed: final dough 500 g flour, 200 g water at 105°F, 20 g salt (2%), no extra yeast, 1,000 g poolish; 0.4 g yeast total (0.04%); poolish 50%.
- Saturday bread at 78% instead of 72%: 780 g water instead of 720 g. Wetter and slacker, so add a couple more folds.
- Whole wheat absorbs more water than white. Change water in small steps and weigh it; 30-40 g makes a big difference.

**Changing flour blends.** Keep the total flour the same and change the mix; every other ingredient is a ratio of total flour.
- Example: Overnight White (p.89) as 700 g white + 200 g whole wheat + 100 g light or dark rye (at room temperature), with water, yeast and salt unchanged (780 g at 90-95°F, 0.8 g, 22 g). The author notes you could add about 20 g more water but keeps it the same because rye is less absorbent.

**Adjusting the schedule.**
- Bulk and proof time depend on the amount of leavening and the dough temperature. The author doesn't recommend speeding them up.
- **Longer bulk:** for an 8-hour bulk on a Saturday bread instead of 5, cut the yeast by about a third and keep the temperatures the same. Take notes and adjust next time.
- **Shaped loaves, plans changed:** put them in the fridge right after shaping, baskets in non-perforated plastic bags, and bake the next morning.
- **About an hour late:** refrigerate the shaped loaves about 50-60 minutes after shaping; they hold for a couple of hours. Bake them cold, no need to warm up.
- **Busy at shaping time:** chill the dough in its tub or as shaped loaves. It keeps fermenting while it cools, so expect some guesswork.
- Cooler bulk or proof can stretch the process and still give excellent bread. Never use under-fermented or under-proofed dough.

**Slow dough (common in winter).**
- Example signs: a dough meant to triple in 5 hours hasn't, or an overnight dough has only doubled when it should be at least 2½ times.
- Fix: a warm spot. The author uses the **oven with the door ajar and the oven light on**. Works for poolish and biga too.
- Keep the tub lid on so the dough doesn't dry out. Check the dough with a probe thermometer and **don't let it go much above 80°F**.

**Levain options.**
- Ratios across the book: hybrid recipes are 20% levain and 0.2% yeast; pure levain recipes are 12% levain with no yeast; the White Flour Warm-Spot Levain Bread (p.185) is 25% levain and 0.1% yeast.
- Warm-Spot Levain, 40% whole wheat version (table as printed): final dough 350 g white, 400 g whole wheat, 625 g water at 80°F, 20 g salt, 1 g yeast (¼ tsp), 425 g levain. Totals 600 g white (60%), 400 g whole wheat (40%), 800 g water (80%), 20 g salt (2%), 1 g yeast (0.1%), levain 25%. The extra 20 g water raises hydration from 78% to 80% for the whole wheat. In-levain column (2nd pass): 250 g flour + 175 g water, so this levain is 70% hydration, not the 80% base. Final-dough flour stays at 750 g; the book's other blend is 100 g rye + 200 g whole wheat + 450 g white.
- With more levain, you can drop the yeast and give the dough longer to rise.

**Liquid levain.**
- Equal weights of water and flour (100% hydration), usually all white flour. Milky, buttery, lactic flavors; overripe can taste slightly fruity.
- Converted from the base levain the day before the mix.
- **Sample schedule:** Day 1 feed at 9 a.m. and 6 p.m.; Day 2 feed at 9 a.m., mix 3 p.m., shape 8 p.m., fridge overnight, bake next morning.

|---|---|---|---|
| Day 1, first | 50 g mature base levain | 250 g | 250 g, 95°F |
| Day 1, second | 250 g from the first feeding | 250 g | 250 g, 85°F |
| Day 2, third | 100 g from the second feeding | 500 g | 500 g, 85-90°F |

- **Pain de Campagne with liquid levain:** final dough 700 g white, 100 g whole wheat, 580 g water at 90-95°F, 21 g salt, 2 g yeast (½ tsp), 400 g levain. Totals 900 g white (90%), 100 g whole wheat (10%), 780 g water (78%), 21 g salt (2.1%), 2 g yeast (0.2%), levain 20%. In-levain column: 200 g flour + 200 g water (2nd pass). **Recipe shorthand and in-process notes.**
- The author records every adjustment while baking, in a pocket notebook, so nothing depends on memory.
- Pizza poolish shorthand (p.225): poolish 500 g flour, 500 g water at 80°F, 0.4 g yeast, 12-14 h at 70°F; no autolyse; final 500 g flour, 250 g water at 105°F, 20 g salt, the poolish; 2 folds; 5-6 h to 2½ times; divide into 5 balls of 350 g; 30-60 min at room temperature, then the fridge.
- In-process notes are times, temperatures and what to change next time (for example, "end mix temp 73°F, use warmer water next time", or for Field Blend, "final mix temp 80°F, use cooler water next time").

## What this changes in the plan (docs/BREAD-PLAN.md)
- **Levain build timing (sourced):** "Build levain" is one feeding 6-9 hours before the final mix. That window is the time between the levain build and the "Is the levain ready?" prompt.
- **Starter defaults:** 80% hydration, whole wheat and white blend, unsalted. The Starter screen can show the 78-90°F (milder, faster) and 55-65°F (more sour) bands as a description.
- **Feeding log prefill (pp.135-136):** the book's daily feed is 100 g kept, 100 g whole wheat, 400 g white, 400 g water at 85-90°F, or the half version. The first feeding can prefill from that, labeled FWSY p.135, and later ones from the last feeding as planned.
- **Tub weight (p.132):** an optional "empty tub weight" on each starter lets the app show the scale reading to stop at (tub plus levain kept). A small, book-sourced convenience.
- **After-feed temperature (p.136):** 78-80°F target right after mixing. Worth an optional "levain temp after feeding" field in the log, next to room temperature.
- **Fridge refresh is two feedings (pp.136-137):** step 1 two days before baking (200 g kept, 95°F water, overnight), step 2 the morning before. For a fridge starter, the bake plan should add both as preparation steps, and the starter reminder lands on step 1. Fridge storage is up to 1 month, but the reminder only needs to fire for the refresh, so the worker's 7-day limit holds when the bake is planned within a week.
- **Readiness prompt timing (p.133):** "Is the levain ready?" lands 7-8 hours after the morning feed, inside the 6-9 h window from the earlier pages.
- **Hybrid vs. pure levain schedules (p.135):** two timing modes for levain bread. Hybrid: feed morning, mix afternoon, shape 5 h later, fridge overnight, bake morning. Pure: feed morning, mix evening, bulk overnight, shape morning, bake 4 h later. Hybrid is the easier first levain release because the fridge proof gives replan slack.
- **Seasonal advice (p.134):** "too sour", "slow in winter" and "warm kitchen" give the bread advice sourced direction: cooler feed water or earlier mix; keep 30-50 g more at the feed; mix 1-2 h earlier.
- **Yeast anchors for yeasted bread:** Saturday White 4 g for about 5 h bulk, Overnight White 0.8 g for 12-14 h. These anchor the existing rate law. Overnight White's 0.8 g matches the pizza overnight dose (FWSY p.220).
- **Fridge proof for straight doughs:** Overnight 40% (12-14 h) and Weeknight White (about 10 h). The plan's "retard window" is not levain-only.
- **First-release candidates:** Saturday White (simplest, one day) or Overnight White. CJ picked Overnight White (2026-11-13); its method pages are transcribed above.
- **Poolish bread:** no autolyse, 80°F poolish water, 105°F final water. The pizza poolish detection stays separate, as the plan says.
- **Built vs. used is settled (ch. 9-10):** every levain recipe builds the full daily feed and uses part of it: 360 g in hybrid recipes, 216 g in Country Brown, 425 g in the Warm-Spot bread (a 70% levain), 400 g with liquid levain. The levain's hydration varies by recipe, so it belongs on the recipe's levain line, not only on the starter. The rest stays in the tub for the next feed or goes to the fridge. The "In levain" column breaks the used amount into flour and water, so only that portion counts toward totals, as the plan already says.
- **Levain percentage means flour, not weight:** the book's levain % is the flour inside the used levain over total flour (20% hybrid, 12% pure, 25% warm-spot). The engine should store the used grams and the starter's hydration, and derive that figure in code.
- **Levain ripeness window:** 7-9 hours on the counter before the final mix (Country Brown step 1a), and 6 hours in the Field Blend shorthand. The readiness prompt can open at the low end.
- **Cool-kitchen levain:** Country Brown says 250-275 g instead of 216 g below 70°F. That's a sourced option for the plan screen, not an automatic change.
- **First levain candidates:** Pain de Campagne (hybrid, fridge proof gives replan slack, full table here) or Overnight Country Brown (pure levain, full table and the first method steps here). Both still need their remaining method pages.
- **Replan slack for straight doughs (essay):** shaped loaves can go in the fridge right away and bake the next morning, or be chilled for a couple of hours and baked cold. That gives "running late" a sourced option for yeasted bread too.
- **Longer bulk:** cut the yeast by about a third to go from 5 to 8 hours of bulk. That's a book figure to check the rate-law estimate against in a test.
- **Slow-dough advice (essay), relevant to pizza too:** oven with the door ajar and the light on, tub lidded, and dough kept no warmer than about 80°F. Commit 72d83af deliberately gave no oven-light figure because none was sourced. This is a sourced method and a sourced ceiling, but still not an oven temperature. CJ's call whether the pizza rise note uses it.
- **Volume cues:** overnight straight dough at least 2½ times; a 5-hour dough triples; Country Blonde triples overnight; pizza poolish dough 2½ times in 5-6 h. These give the checklist "how big should it be" text.
- **Pizza check:** the app's Forkish Overnight Poolish preset (presets.ts L65-102) already has 0.4 g yeast, 80°F poolish water and 105°F final water, matching the p.225 shorthand. The 70% hydration variant (200 g final water, one fold) could be a sourced edit, not a new preset.
- **Recipe shorthand = the batch "actual" layer:** the author's in-process notes (times, end-of-mix temperature, what to change next time) are what the locked-batch actuals and notes already hold. An "end-of-mix dough temperature" field is the one gap worth adding for bread.
- **Liquid levain:** a starter at 100% hydration. The starter's hydration field covers it; the three-feeding conversion could become a starter "convert" preset later. Not first release.
